# Watermark Identifiability — An Empirical Study on Claude's Own Output

*A companion experiment to [`../ai-content-watermarking.md`](../ai-content-watermarking.md).
Every number below is computed by [`analyze_watermark.py`](./analyze_watermark.py)
(pure-Python, deterministic, seed `20260811`) over a corpus generated by a fan-out of Claude
subagents. No statistic in this document was produced by a language model asserting it — only by code.*

---

## The question (stated honestly)

The literal request was "identify the underlying mechanism that is used to watermark my own output,
with statistical confidence." That exact question is **not answerable from output text alone**, and
pretending otherwise would manufacture false confidence. So this study answers the precise,
*answerable* version:

> **Can a keyed generative (green-list-style) text watermark be identified or recovered from a
> model's output text alone — without the secret key, the exact scheme, and the tokenizer?**

The answer, with statistical confidence and a positive control, is **no** — and the experiment
shows *why*, and exactly what you *do* recover instead (style, not watermark).

> ⚠️ **Scope guardrail (baked in after adversarial review).** This is *keyless non-identifiability
> within one watermark family*. It is **not** a test of "is Claude watermarked," and **not** evidence
> that the output is un-watermarked. A watermark is designed to be invisible; **absence of evidence is
> not evidence of absence.** Other families (Aaronson/Gumbel, distortion-free) are invisible to this
> detector by construction.

---

## Corpus

A workflow fanned out **8 Claude subagents**, each writing ~1,200–1,500 words of
natural, high-entropy prose (essays, fiction, nature writing — genres where a watermark would be
*strongest*, maximizing our ability to detect one). Totals:

- **10,935 word tokens**, 2,759 types (type–token ratio 0.2523, hapax 65%)
- 483 sentences, mean length 22.64 words (σ 16.61, burstiness CV 0.734)

## Part 1 — What "scan the outputs for patterns" actually recovers: **stylometry**

This is the layer people mistake for a watermark. It is real, measurable, and **present with or
without any watermark** — which is exactly why it cannot reveal a keyed mark.

| Feature | Value |
|---|---|
| Mean word length | 4.394 chars |
| Function-word fraction | 0.4128 |
| Em-dash / 1000 words | 1.006 |
| Comma / 1000 words | 70.416 |
| Semicolon / 1000 words | 1.555 |
| Colon / 1000 words | 1.738 |
| Sentence-length burstiness (CV) | 0.734 |

Top content unigrams: `its`(54), `have`(53), `had`(46), `one`(42), `light`(42), `out`(40), `has`(37), `up`(35), `blue`(35), `there`(34)
Top bigrams: `and the`(58), `of the`(54), `in the`(44), `it is`(34), `of a`(32), `on the`(29), `and a`(25), `is a`(25)

**This is a style fingerprint, not a watermark.** It would look much the same whether or not the text
carries a keyed mark, and none of it lets you recover a key. Post-hoc "AI detectors" key on features
like these — and that is a *different, unreliable layer* from cryptographic provenance.

## Part 2 — The core result: a keyed watermark is invisible without the key

We reproduce a **word-level Kirchenbauer green-list detector** (γ=0.5, one-sided *z*, keyed
blake2b). For a key *k*, a token is "green" iff `hash(k, prev, cur) < γ`; watermarked text
over-produces green tokens, giving a large *z*. Without the key you must *guess* it.

### 2a. Null: scanning random keys on the real corpus

Trying **4,000 random keys** against the (un-watermarked-by-this-scheme) Claude corpus:

| Detector | *z* mean | *z* stdev | *z* max (strongest false positive) | frac keys with *z*>4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| All tokens (naïve) | -0.0115 | **1.858** | 5.662 | 0.01325 |
| Distinct n-grams (independence-corrected) | -0.011 | **1.0273** | 3.025 | 0.0 |

**Why two rows (an honesty fix the adversarial reviewer forced):** the naïve *z*-test assumes i.i.d.
Bernoulli trials, but seeding on the previous word makes every *repeated bigram* a perfectly
correlated green/red cluster, so the empirical null is **overdispersed** (stdev 1.858, not 1).
Scoring **distinct** n-grams restores independence (stdev 1.0273 ≈ 1). Lesson: **set
thresholds from the empirical null, not from Gaussian theory.** The strongest false positive over
4,000 random keys was only *z*=5.662.

### 2b. Positive control: inject a known-key watermark, then try to detect it

We synthesize watermarked text (δ logit-bias on green tokens under a **known** key `k*`, sampled from
the corpus's own bigram statistics) and run the detector three ways:

| Watermark | green frac | *z* with **true key + correct scheme** | *z* with **wrong keys** (mean±σ) | best decoy *z* | *z* with **true key + WRONG scheme (h=2)** | gap (true − best decoy) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| δ=0.5 | 0.603 | **18.5** | -0.00 ± 1.74 | 4.7 | 1.1 | 13.8 |
| δ=1.0 | 0.699 | **35.5** | -0.04 ± 1.79 | 6.3 | -0.8 | 29.2 |
| δ=2.0 | 0.833 | **59.6** | -0.07 ± 1.91 | 5.4 | -0.0 | 54.1 |

Reading this table:

- **With the key *and* the scheme** → the watermark is unmistakable (*z* in the tens; the gap over the
  strongest wrong-key decoy is in the double digits, see the last column).
- **Wrong keys** → collapse to the empirical null (mean ≈ 0). The mark is **invisible without the key**.
- **True key but wrong scheme** (detector assumes 2-word context instead of 1) → *z* collapses to
  null too. So you need the **scheme and tokenizer**, not merely the key.

This is the crux: identification requires **key ∧ scheme ∧ tokenizer**, none of which is present in
the output.

### 2c. Why brute force cannot rescue you

A real key space is cryptographically large (e.g. 2^128). Random/brute-forced keys yield the empirical null above; the one true key is a single unsearchable point, and any threshold loose enough to admit a weak watermark also admits many false positives. Hence the scheme/key is NOT identifiable from outputs alone.

- Empirically, strongest false-positive *z* over 4,000 keys = **5.662**.
- Theoretical one-sided tail at *z*>4 ≈ **3.17e-05** — *labeled
  theoretical and contingent*: Theoretical tail assumes i.i.d. Bernoulli, which repeated n-grams violate (see null_distinct vs null_full). Use the EMPIRICAL null for thresholds. Resolving a 3.2e-05 rate empirically would need ~1e5-1e6 keys, not 4000.

At a real key space (e.g. 2¹²⁸), the one true key is a single unsearchable point, and any threshold
loose enough to admit a *weak* watermark also admits a flood of false positives. **The scheme/key is
not identifiable from outputs.**

---

## Part 3 — Where a watermark could even live (capacity)

A watermark can only bias tokens the model was *free* to choose. Entropy proxies on the corpus:

- Unigram entropy: **9.058 bits/word**
- Bigram conditional entropy: **3.618 bits/word**
- Fraction of near-deterministic (>0.9) positions: **0.1717**

High-entropy prose (like this corpus) is the *best case* for carrying a watermark; low-entropy text
(code, quotations, terse facts) can carry little or none. This is an information-theoretic ceiling,
not an implementation detail.

---

## Precise, defensible conclusion

> From output text alone — without the secret key, the exact scheme (context width, hashing, gamma), and the model's tokenization — a Kirchenbauer-style keyed green-list watermark is NOT identifiable: random and brute-forced keys yield a null indistinguishable from unwatermarked text, and the key space is too large to search without false positives swamping any true hit. The positive control shows the signal is unmistakable WITH the key+scheme (z_true >> best decoy) and vanishes without EITHER the key (wrong-key scan) OR the scheme (h2 mismatch). This is keyless non-identifiability within ONE watermark family; it is NOT evidence that outputs are unwatermarked, and it says nothing about other families (Aaronson/Gumbel, distortion-free) invisible to this detector by construction. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

In one line: **you cannot read a keyed watermark off the outputs; the thing you *can* measure
(style) is not the watermark; and the honest finding is non-identifiability, never "clean."**

## How the adversarial review changed the design

An independent reviewer agent stress-tested the plan *before* the numbers were computed. It changed
the study in five concrete ways, all reflected above:

1. **Thresholds from the empirical null, not Gaussian theory** (repeated-n-gram overdispersion).
2. **Added the distinct-n-gram detector** to demonstrate *why* the null is overdispersed.
3. **Added the scheme-mismatch test** (true key + wrong context width → collapse): the missing
   ingredient is the *scheme + tokenizer*, not only the key.
4. **Labeled the deep-tail (3e-5) number as theoretical-and-contingent**, since 4,000 keys
   cannot empirically resolve it.
5. **Hard guardrail against overclaiming**: non-identifiability ≠ "unwatermarked."

## Core problem in one line
The design conflates *"no green-list signal is recoverable without the key"* with *"no watermark is present."* Only the first is demonstrable here, and even that is demonstrable only for one watermark family at one granularity. Keep every conclusion inside those walls.

## 1. Is the NULL a valid demonstration? Partly — but it is closer to a tautology than evidence, and it has real confounds.

- **It cannot distinguish the two worlds it needs to distinguish.** "Unwatermarked" and "watermarked under a secret key k_secret not in your 2000 random draws" both produce z ~ N(0,1) for random keys. The key space is ~2^128; P(a random draw hits k_secret) ≈ 0. So the null is guaranteed by construction regardless of whether Claude is watermarked. A result that is identical under both hypotheses is not evidence about which hypothesis is true — it is evidence about hash functions.
- **The persuasive content is the key-space/search argument, not the histogram.** The 2000-key null is a calibration sanity check. The actual identifiability claim rests on: the true key is one unsearchable point, and any brute-force threshold loose enough to admit a weakly-watermarked corpus also admits a flood of false positives. Foreground that; the histogram is decorative.
- **Independence is violated, so "expect N(0,1)" may be wrong.** The z-statistic assumes T i.i.d. Bernoulli(γ) trials. But seeding on w_{t-1} makes greenness a deterministic function of the bigram (w_{t-1}, w_t); every repeated bigram is a perfectly correlated (all-green or all-red) cluster. Natural text is bigram-heavy, so the effective trial count << T and the empirical null is **overdispersed** (SD > 1, heavier tails). Comparing to a theoretical N(0,1) will then mislabel chance |z|>4 events. Fix: set thresholds from the *empirical* null (mean/SD/quantiles of the 2000 keys), not from Gaussian theory.
- **K=2000 cannot support the 3.2e-5 you later quote.** Expected |z|>4 events in 2000 keys ≈ 0.06–0.13, so "≈none" is a foregone conclusion that says nothing about the 3.2e-5 tail. Empirically resolving a 3.2e-5 rate needs ~10^5–10^6 keys. As written, 3.2e-5 is pure Gaussian extrapolation that the overdispersion concern directly undercuts — an internal inconsistency. Either raise K by ~1000x or label 3.2e-5 explicitly as theoretical-and-contingent-on-calibration.
- **Two internal spec bugs to fix before running:** (a) `hash(...) mod 1` is identically 0 for integer hashes → every word "green" → degenerate huge z; you mean `hash/HASH_MAX ∈ [0,1) < γ`. (b) "|z|>4" (two-sided, 6.3e-5) is notationally inconsistent with the quoted 3.2e-5 (one-sided). A watermark only *raises* green count, so one-sided z>4 is correct — then write it as `z>4`, not `|z|>4`.

## 2. Is the POSITIVE CONTROL sound? It validates the pipeline, not the hypothesis — and it is circular as specified.

- **Self-consistency trap.** If you synthesize the watermark with the *same* hash, γ, w_{t-1} seeding, and tokenization the detector uses, then "k* gives huge z, wrong keys give 0" is guaranteed. It proves the detector detects its own construction — not that a *real* watermark (different context width h, token-level not word-level, or a different family entirely) would register.
- **The genuinely on-thesis part** is "wrong keys give ~0": that is the real result — a watermark is visible *only* with the key. But make the watermark realistic or it tempts overclaim. Force-greening tokens gives z=200 and fluency collapse; a real logit-bias (δ boost) sampler yields a realistic green fraction (~0.6) and a modest z, which is the honest operating regime.
- **What would make it convincing:**
  1. **Scheme-mismatch collapse** — watermark under one scheme (word h=1), detect assuming another (h=2, or token-level). Show detection dies. This is the single most decision-relevant plot for the thesis: you're missing the *scheme and tokenizer*, not just the key.
  2. **Power/ROC surface** over (δ, γ, T): the minimum strength and corpus length at which detection becomes reliable — i.e., the sensitivity floor.
  3. **Blind mixture test**: hand the detector a set of candidate keys, only one real, plus unwatermarked decoys; report TPR/FPR without telling it k*.
  4. **Token vs word**: real watermarks key on BPE tokens; a whitespace-word detector cannot reproduce the partition even with the true key. Demonstrate this gap explicitly.

Do **not** let it read as "Claude has no watermark," "outputs are clean," or "scanning proves nothing is there." Stylometry (Part 1) especially cannot rule a watermark out — a watermark is designed to be stylometrically invisible; present that section as orthogonal, not as absence-of-watermark.

**Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.** The null result is a near-mathematical certainty for random keys whether or not a watermark exists, because the true key is one point in an unsearchable space and other watermark families/tokenizations don't register on a word-level green-list test at all.
