The bend comes upon you before you hear it, a slow left-handed swing of water where the river, having run straight and businesslike through the water meadows, seems all at once to change its mind. Here the current leans its whole weight against the far bank, gnawing a red-brown cliff of clay and root, while on the near side it loses conviction and lets fall whatever it has been carrying — a long tongue of gravel and pale sand that in most months lies exposed to the sky. Between the undercut and the shoal the water deepens and darkens and turns upon itself, and it is this turning, this patient argument between erosion and deposit, that has made the place what it is. To sit above it through a whole circling year is to watch a single sentence of the river being written and unwritten and written again.

In March the thaw arrives not as a trickle but as a brown insurgency. The river swells the colour of milky tea, muscular and loud, and the bend takes the brunt of it. Meltwater off the hills comes down freighted with silt and last autumn's drowned leaves, and the whole channel seems to hurry, shouldering aside the reeds, flooding the gravel bar until only a grey hummock of it shows. The alder that leans from the cut bank stands to its ankles in the flood, and around its submerged roots the current plaits and unplaits itself in ceaseless ropes. Then, almost overnight, the violence relents. The water clears from the bottom up, the way a held breath is let go, and the first real warmth of the year comes trembling off it in a thin haze at dawn. Marsh marigolds ignite along the wet margins, a raw and buttery yellow. A dipper, that small brown fanatic, works the riffle at the head of the bend, curtseying on a stone and then walking calmly under the surface as though the laws that govern other birds have been suspended in its favour. Frog-song wells up from the ditch behind the willows, a wet mechanical churring that goes on all night. Everything is provisional, sodden, beginning.

By June the bend has settled into its long green complacency. The river has dropped and slowed and warmed, and the water over the shoal runs so clear that the gravel looks close enough to touch, though it lies a hand's-breadth deeper than the eye insists. Now the place is loud with insects rather than water. Damselflies, blue as struck matches, stitch the air above the reed-tops; mayflies rise in their brief and tragic thousands, dance, couple, and fall spent onto the film of the surface, where the trout are waiting. You can watch a good fish hold in the seam between fast and slow water, finning, drifting up to sip a fly with a dimple that spreads and is gone, sinking back to its station with hardly a movement wasted. The cut bank, drying now, is a tenement of holes: the neat round door of a water vole, the deeper burrow where a kingfisher nests, and once — a jewel flung — the bird itself, a scald of turquoise and rust downstream so fast the mind registers it only after it has passed. Willows lean their long hair into the current. The meadow behind is chest-high with grasses gone to seed, meadowsweet and vetch and the sweet hay-smell of it baking, and the river slides through all this abundance with the unhurried authority of something that will outlast every green thing watching it.

Autumn undoes the bend slowly and then all at once. Through September the light lengthens and goes gold, striking low across the water so that every ripple casts a small travelling shadow, and the alder leaves begin to let go, spinning down to lie in slow flotillas on the eddy behind the point bar. The trout darken and grow bold and greedy, laying on fat against the lean months. Salmon and sea-trout, if this is that kind of river, come up on the autumn spates, and you may see one turn in the deep water below the cut — a great muscled bar of silver going the wrong way, upstream, against everything, driven by a compass no one has ever found. The reeds bleach from green to fawn to a brittle biscuit brown and rattle in the wind like something applauding. Morning mist stands waist-deep over the meadow, and out of it the alarm of a heron, a prehistoric croak, as the bird unfolds itself from the shoal and rows away on slow grey wings. Leaves clog the eddy, sink, and begin their long return to silt. The river, running higher now on the first rains of October, resumes its old work at the cliff face, and clods of clay slump into the flood with a soft collapsing sound, taking a fringe of grass down with them. What summer built, autumn quietly repossesses.

Then the cold closes its hand. In the deep of winter the bend is stripped to its bones, and this, oddly, is when its architecture shows clearest — the geometry of the meander laid bare, the steep scoured face of the outer bank, the low pale sweep of gravel opposite, the whole logic of the turning made visible without the distraction of leaves. On the hardest mornings a lace of ice forms in the slack water behind the shoal, thin plates that grow inward from the margins and knock softly against each other where the current still nudges them. The main channel never freezes; it runs black and smoking between white banks, too fast and too full of its own warmth to be caught. Frost furs every reed-stem and every dead umbel of hogweed until the whole margin looks dusted in sugar, and the low sun, when it comes, does not so much warm the scene as gild it. Tracks appear on the snowy point bar overnight and are gone by noon: the neat purposeful stitching of an otter, the splayed hand of a moorhen, the drag-mark where something was carried. The willows are bare and dark and dripping, their reflection wavering in the black water like ink not yet dry. Sound carries strangely. A robin's thin winter song, the clatter of a startled snipe zigzagging up from the ditch, the endless undertone of the river talking to its stones — these come to you clean and close across the cold air, unmuffled by any leaf. Life has not left the bend so much as gone down into it, into the burrows and the silt and the deep unfrozen pool, waiting.

And it is waiting that the place teaches, finally, if it teaches anything. The bend is never the same twice and never anything but itself. The gravel that lay bare in June rode down on the March flood from somewhere upstream and will ride on again next spring toward some other shore; the clay that the current is patiently eating from the outer bank is being laid down, grain by grain, on the inner curve of the next bend below, where in a hundred years there may be a water meadow full of marigolds. The river is not a thing but a doing. It carries the mountains to the sea a handful at a time and asks no one's leave, and the seasons are only the different weathers of its long indifference, spring's insurgency and summer's ease and autumn's slow repossession and the stripped patient dark of the cold — all of it one motion, one bend of bright water endlessly turning, wearing the land into the shape of its own leaving.
