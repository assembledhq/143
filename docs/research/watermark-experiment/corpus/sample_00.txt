There is an hour the city keeps for itself, and if you want it you have to steal it, because no one hands it over willingly. I have been going out into that hour for a few years now, not every night but often enough that my body has learned to wake without alarm somewhere around four, the way you wake in a house you grew up in, knowing which stair creaks. I lie there a moment and listen to nothing, and then I get up and put on yesterday's clothes and go down into the street while the rest of the household breathes on without me. It is not insomnia, though it began as insomnia. It has become something closer to appetite.

The first thing you notice is the color, or rather the absence of the colors you expect. Streetlights leave everything a sodium bruise, that orange that isn't quite orange, and the shadows they throw are hard and theatrical, so that a parked bicycle becomes a spider and a mailbox becomes a squat little sentry. Above the rooflines the sky has not yet decided what it is going to be. It is the deep used-up blue of a chalkboard wiped with a wet rag, still holding the ghost of everything that was written on it and erased. You could call it black but it isn't black; black is a country the sky visited hours ago and has already begun the slow business of leaving. What I am walking through is the leaving.

The streets are wet even when it hasn't rained. Something about the temperature dropping, the dew coming down on the cars and the cold iron of the railings, so that the whole city looks freshly rinsed, as if a stagehand came through with a hose while the audience was out in the lobby. My footsteps are enormous. In daylight you never hear yourself walk; the sound is swallowed by ten thousand other sounds, buses and conversation and the electric hum of commerce. Now each step comes back to me off the shuttered storefronts, and I find I walk more carefully, the way you lower your voice in an empty church without deciding to, out of a courtesy to the silence that feels almost superstitious.

You meet people, but they are a particular kind of people, and there is a freemasonry among them that daylight does not permit. The bakers, first of all, their windows the only warm rectangles on the block, flour to the elbow, moving through the fog of their own ovens like figures in a painting whose subject the museum has forgotten. The man hosing down the fish market, rubber apron shining. A nurse at a bus stop with the particular stillness of someone whose shift has emptied her out completely, holding a paper cup she is not drinking from. A drunk, sometimes, negotiating peace with a lamppost. And the ones like me, walkers, who nod as they pass because we have caught each other in the act of wanting the same forbidden thing, and there is nothing to say about it, and so we say nothing, and the nod carries all of it. In the daytime we would be strangers and would owe each other only the ordinary blindness that keeps a city livable. Out here we are something else, something without a name, fellow trespassers in the hour that belongs to no one.

I think what I am after, on these walks, is the city with its performance suspended. All day it is doing something, this place, all of us are, we are producing and buying and hurrying and being seen, and the buildings themselves seem to strain upward with the effort of it. But at four in the morning the effort stops. The office towers stand there dumb and dark, and you understand for the first time that they are just stone and glass, that no one lives inside their ambition, that the whole roaring machine is only a habit we agree to resume each morning and could, conceivably, decline. There is a great tenderness in seeing a thing off its guard. The city asleep is like a stern relative caught dozing in an armchair, mouth open, suddenly and unbearably mortal, and you love it in a way its waking dignity would never have allowed.

The mind does a strange thing in that emptiness. It unclenches. All day my thoughts run in their well-worn grooves, the small anxieties filing past on schedule like commuters, the same three worries taking turns at the front of the queue. But walking, in the dark, with nowhere I have to be and no one watching me arrive, the grooves lose their grip. Thoughts I did not know I was carrying float up and hang in front of me for inspection. I have solved things on these walks, or believed I had, only to find the solution had evaporated by breakfast, which does not trouble me, because I have come to suspect the solving was never the point. The point was the loosening. The point was to remember that I am a creature who walks, an animal in the oldest sense, before I am an employee or a correspondent or a name on a hundred lists.

There is a bridge I aim for, most mornings, though I do not always reach it. From the middle of it you can see the water going about its ancient work, indifferent, carrying the lights of the city in long smeared ribbons, breaking them and mending them, breaking them again. I stand there and lean on the cold rail and I feel the particular loneliness that is not sadness, that is in fact almost the opposite of sadness, the loneliness of being one small warm thing awake in a vast cool sleeping world and finding that you do not mind, that you are, against all the day's evidence, enough. The river does not need me to watch it. That is precisely why I like to watch it. To be unnecessary to something so large is a relief the day never offers.

And then it starts, the thing I came for and always half forget is coming. It is not dramatic. That is what nobody tells you about dawn in a city: it does not arrive like a trumpet, it arrives like a rumor. The sky in the east goes from that used-up blue to something gray and undecided, and then a thin dilution begins at the very bottom, a paleness, as if someone very far away were slowly turning up a dial they were not sure they were allowed to touch. The hard sodium shadows begin to soften and lose conviction. A bird, one bird, tries a note and thinks better of it, and then tries again, and then the others agree that yes, apparently, it is time, and the chorus comes up out of the plane trees like something that has been waiting all night for permission. The first bus sighs past with its handful of gray passengers. A light comes on in an upper window. Somewhere a metal shutter goes up with that sound like a wave breaking on gravel, and the day, which had been so beautifully, so mercifully off, switches itself back on.

I always feel a small grief at that moment, and a small gratitude, and they are so tangled I cannot separate them. The private hour is ending. The city is remembering its performance and will shortly ask me to remember mine. But I have had it, the thing itself, the world with its mask down, and I carry it home through streets that are filling now with the ordinary light of an ordinary Tuesday, and no one I pass on their way to work would ever guess that I have just come back from somewhere they cannot go, a country that exists only between the last streetlight and the first bird, and that closes its borders the instant anyone thinks to look for it in the sun.
