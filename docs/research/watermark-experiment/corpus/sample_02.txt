Look up on a clear afternoon and you are staring straight into a magic trick that has been running, without a single intermission, for four and a half billion years. The sky is blue. Not painted blue, not blue because it is built out of some blue material the way a wall is blue because someone rolled blue paint across it, but blue for a subtle physical reason that took clever people several centuries to finally pin down. And here is the lovely part: the very same reason, turned inside out, is why the setting sun bleeds orange and red at the end of the day. Once you understand how the trick is done, you cannot un-understand it, and the sky quietly becomes a physics demonstration staged above your head every single day, free of charge.

Begin with the light itself, because everything follows from what light actually is. Sunlight looks white, or maybe a thin butter-yellow, but that whiteness is a costume. What we casually call white light is in truth an entire crowd of colors travelling shoulder to shoulder: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, and on into violet. You have caught them apart before, even if you did not think about it much. A rainbow does it with raindrops. The beveled edge of a drinking glass throws a little smear of spectrum onto the tablecloth. A soap bubble wears the colors like an oily rainbow skin. A glass prism, most famously, takes a narrow beam of sunlight and fans it into a ribbon of every hue. The prism is not adding anything. It is only separating out colors that were riding inside the beam the whole time, packed so tightly together that your eye adds them up and reports the total as no color at all.

Now for the idea that unlocks the whole puzzle. Light travels as a wave, and each color corresponds to a wave of a slightly different length. Red light is the long, lazy, loping wave, with crests spaced comparatively far apart. Blue and violet are the short, quick, choppy ones, their crests bunched close together. The differences are absurdly tiny by everyday standards, measured in billionths of a meter, but they are the entire reason the sky has a color. Red waves are nearly twice as long as violet ones, and that near-doubling matters enormously, as you will see, because the effect we care about is savagely sensitive to wavelength.

Between you and the sun sits the atmosphere, which is not empty. It is a vast ocean of gas, overwhelmingly nitrogen and oxygen, and it is thronged with an unthinkable number of molecules, each one fantastically smaller than the wavelength of the light streaming through it. When a wave of sunlight sweeps past one of these little molecules, it jostles the molecule's electrons, and the molecule promptly re-radiates that light back out in some new direction. Physicists call this scattering, and the tidy mental picture is that each molecule catches a sliver of incoming light and flings it off at a fresh angle, like a buoy that gets nudged by a passing swell and sends out ripples of its own.

Here is the decisive twist. Tiny molecules are far, far better at scattering the short, choppy blue waves than the long, loping red ones. The relationship is brutally lopsided: the amount of scattering climbs as the wavelength shrinks, and it does so steeply, roughly in proportion to one over the wavelength raised to the fourth power. Do not let the arithmetic scare you; the punchline is simply that halving the wavelength does not double the scattering, it multiplies it by about sixteen. Run the real numbers for blue against red and you find that blue light is scattered something like nine or ten times more vigorously than red. Red mostly ignores the molecules and barrels straight through the air toward the ground. Blue, by contrast, gets caught, flung sideways, caught again, flung again, ricocheting around the sky like a pinball that never quite settles.

So picture the whole scene. Sunlight pours into the atmosphere. The red, orange, and yellow largely keep going in a straight line. The blue gets scattered out of that straight beam and sprayed across the entire dome of the sky, bouncing from molecule to molecule until it is arriving at your eyes from every direction at once — from overhead, from the horizon, from the patch of air above a distant hill. When you tilt your head back and look at a stretch of sky nowhere near the sun, the light reaching you from that empty blue expanse is scattered sunlight, and it is dominated by blue. The sky glows blue because the air is, in effect, a screen lit up by the blue light it has stolen out of the sunbeam. This also quietly explains why the sky is bright at all rather than pitch black with a blazing sun stuck in it. Out in space, where there is no air to scatter anything, that is exactly what astronauts see: a fierce white sun hanging in absolute darkness. Our blue sky is the glow of our atmosphere doing its scattering.

A sharp question usually surfaces right about here. If shorter waves scatter even more powerfully than blue, and violet is shorter than blue, why isn't the sky violet? Good instinct, and the answer is a two-part collaboration between the sun and your own eyes. First, the sun simply pumps out somewhat less violet light than blue to begin with, so there is less of it in the mix. Second, and more importantly, human eyes are not neutral judges. We see color through three kinds of cone cells in the retina, and their combined sensitivity leans toward blue and green while trailing off in the violet. So even though violet is being scattered enthusiastically up there, we are half-blind to it and swimming in blue, and the brain files the whole overhead glow under a confident, cheerful blue. A creature with different eyes, hovering under the same sky, might honestly insist it looked violet.

Which brings us, at last, to the sunset, and to the pleasure of realizing it needs almost no new physics at all. Through the middle of the day the sun is more or less overhead, and its light punches down through a relatively thin slab of atmosphere to reach you. But as the sun sinks toward the horizon, its light comes in at a low, grazing slant, and to get to your eyes it must now plow through a vastly longer corridor of air — hundreds of miles of it instead of a few dozen. Over that enormous slanting journey, the scattering that merely tinted the noon sky blue gets carried to its logical extreme. Blue is scattered out of the beam early and thoroughly, stripped away long before the light arrives. Green and yellow gradually go too. What survives the long gauntlet and finally reaches your eye is the stubborn long-wavelength light that resisted scattering all along: the oranges and the deep reds. The setting sun looks red not because it has changed, but because the atmosphere has picked its light clean of everything else, leaving only the reds standing.

The blue sky and the red sunset, then, are not two separate wonders but two faces of one single process. The blue is the light that got scattered away; the red is the light that was left behind after the scattering had its way. They are debit and credit in the same account. Throw extra particles into the air — dust from a far-off desert, smoke from a distant fire, haze after a volcano flings ash into the high atmosphere — and you tilt the balance further, which is exactly why the most unforgettable, blood-orange, sky-on-fire sunsets so often follow a fire or an eruption somewhere over the horizon.

So the next time you catch a sunset and feel that reflexive little tug of awe, know that you are watching the identical trick that painted the sky blue at lunchtime, only run at full length until it burns. The color was never in the sky or in the sun. It was in the journey the light had to take to reach you.
