A photograph is a strange kind of promise, and like most promises it is kept only partially. It swears to hold a moment still, to arrest the flow of things, and in one sense it does exactly this: here is the light as it fell on a particular afternoon, here is the exact angle of a shoulder, the precise dishevelment of hair that no one present thought worth noticing at the time. And yet the very fidelity of the photograph is what makes it lie. Because the moment it claims to preserve was never still. It was already leaving when the shutter closed on it. What the image holds is not the moment but the corpse of the moment, embalmed and posed, wearing the face of the living thing it used to be.

We tend to speak of memory and photography as allies, as though the second were simply an external hard drive for the first, a way of offloading the burden of remembering onto silver and paper and, now, onto the vast indifferent server farms that hum somewhere in the desert. But the relationship is more adversarial than that, more like the rivalry between siblings who resemble each other too closely. Memory is fluid, revisionary, promiscuous in its associations; it rewrites itself every time it is summoned, so that each act of remembering is also an act of gentle forgery. A photograph, by contrast, does not change. It is stubborn. It insists. And in its insistence it slowly overwrites the softer, truer, more mutable thing that was the memory itself. Ask anyone to recall a childhood scene that was heavily photographed, and what they will produce for you is not the scene but the snapshot: the frozen grin, the particular composition the adult with the camera chose. The photograph has eaten the memory and now sits in its chair, wearing its clothes.

There is a peculiar grief in this that we rarely name. We imagine we are saving our lives when we photograph them, but we may be spending them instead, trading the living currency of experience for the paper money of the image. The tourist who watches the cathedral through a screen, framing and adjusting, has in some sense declined to see the cathedral at all; he has deferred the seeing to a later date that may never arrive, or that will arrive only as a scroll through a thousand near-identical files, none of which was ever really looked at, first or last. The image was supposed to be a supplement to the experience. It became a substitute for it. And the substitute, being permanent, outlasts and finally replaces the thing it was meant merely to assist.

But I do not want to be only mournful about this, because the photograph does something that memory alone cannot, and it is worth being precise about the gift as well as the theft. Memory has no respect for the trivial. It keeps what it deems significant and discards the rest with a ruthlessness that feels, in retrospect, like carelessness. The photograph is more democratic, more indiscriminate. It keeps the background. It keeps the ugly lamp, the outdated calendar on the wall, the brand of soda that has since been discontinued, the exact weather, the incidental stranger caught mid-stride at the edge of the frame who has no idea, to this day, that he is preserved forever in the corner of someone else's wedding. These details, worthless at the moment of capture, ripen into treasure with age. What we photograph for its foreground we often come to love for its background, because the background is where time does its most honest work, quietly, without posing. The face we meant to capture has aged along with us and so seems less changed; it is the wallpaper, the haircut, the width of a necktie that suddenly announces how far we have traveled from that country we can no longer visit.

This is the deep paradox at the heart of the photograph: it is an instrument for measuring time that works only because it does not itself move. A ruler must be rigid to measure length. The photograph is rigid in time, a fixed rod laid against the flowing river, and we read our own velocity by watching how far we have drifted from the mark it holds. A living face tells us nothing about aging because it ages too gradually and too continuously for us to perceive; we look in the mirror each morning and see no change, because change at that resolution is invisible. But set beside a photograph of ourselves at twenty, the mirror suddenly speaks. The photograph is not a window into the past so much as a benchmark against which the present measures its distance from the past, and the emotion it produces, that vertiginous ache, is nothing other than the felt experience of that distance, the sudden vertigo of realizing how far one has fallen from a height one did not know one was standing on.

The philosophers of an earlier century worried a great deal about whether the photograph could be called art, whether a machine could make beauty, whether the absence of the human hand disqualified it from the company of painting. These now seem like the wrong questions, or at least like questions that missed the real strangeness of the thing. The scandal of the photograph is not aesthetic but ontological. It is that a photograph is not a picture of a thing but a trace of it, a physical residue, light that actually bounced off a real body and was actually caught. A painting of my grandmother is a report about her, filtered through a mind. A photograph of my grandmother is a fossil of her, the literal print left by photons that touched her skin and then touched the film. She is gone, and yet something of her light is still here, having made the journey from her face to my eyes across the impassable border of her death. No wonder the old cultures feared the camera stole the soul. It does not steal the soul, but it does something almost as uncanny: it detains the light, holds it hostage against the day when the body that shed it will be dust, and then releases it, unchanged, into a future that body will never see.

And so we are haunted, gently, continuously, by these rectangles. The dead look out at us with the full confidence of the living, unaware of what we know and they did not, that this ordinary Tuesday, this picnic, this restless afternoon, was among their last. We possess a terrible knowledge the photograph itself does not contain: the knowledge of what came after. The image is innocent of its own future. It smiles. It has no idea. This is the source of the almost unbearable poignancy of old photographs of people we know to have suffered, to have died young, to have walked unknowing toward some catastrophe just outside the frame and just after the shutter. We read the photograph forward and backward at once, seeing both the moment as it was, weightless and open, and the moment as it became, sealed and finished, and the collision of these two readings is precisely where the feeling lives.

Perhaps this is what the photograph finally teaches, if we let it: not how to defeat time, which cannot be done, but how to hold two irreconcilable truths in a single glance. That the moment was, and that the moment is no more. That the light was real, and that the body it touched is gone. That we can preserve everything and save nothing, that the very act of clutching at time leaves us holding only its shed skin, beautiful and empty, warm-looking and cold to the touch. We keep the photographs anyway. We keep them because the alternative, to let the light go entirely, is unthinkable, and because there is a strange fidelity in tending these small rectangular graves, dusting them, arranging them, returning to them, as though by looking hard enough we might yet climb back through the frame into the afternoon that made us, the afternoon that is still there, still lit, still waiting, and utterly, permanently out of reach.
