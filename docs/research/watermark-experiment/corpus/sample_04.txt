The road gave out about four kilometers below the village, dissolving from cracked asphalt into a rutted track of pale clay that the driver navigated with a fatalism I found almost soothing. He had said perhaps a dozen words since the valley floor, most of them concerning the weather, which he distrusted, and the brakes, which he distrusted more. When the van could go no farther he simply stopped, pointed his chin at a footpath switchbacking up through terraced fields of buckwheat, and named a price that seemed to include his forgiveness for my having come at all. I shouldered my pack and climbed. The air thinned into something bright and mineral, scented with woodsmoke and the resinous tang of juniper, and by the time the first stone houses came into view I was breathing like a bellows and grinning like an idiot, sweat cooling at the small of my back.

Kharsang, the village is called, though every map I had consulted spelled it differently, as if cartographers too had struggled to catch their breath at this altitude and settled for approximations. Perhaps forty households cling to the mountainside here, their slate roofs weighted with river stones against the wind, their walls a warm ochre that seemed to hoard the last of the afternoon light. An old woman winnowing grain on a flat roof watched me pass without expression, tossing her wooden tray so the chaff lifted away in a golden veil and the heavier kernels rained back down. A dog followed me the length of the single lane, keeping a diplomatic three paces, and then, having satisfied itself of something, peeled off and vanished between two houses. I had arranged nothing. I had only a name scrawled on a napkin, Dorje, and the vague assurance of a hostel-keeper two valleys back that this Dorje would find me before I found him.

He did. I had barely set down my pack in the little square, if you could dignify with that word the patch of packed earth where three lanes converged around a chorten and a mani wall of carved stones, when a wiry man of indeterminate age materialized at my elbow and took my hand in both of his. His face was a topography of weather, seamed and burnished, and his English arrived in eager, slightly antique bursts, as though he had learned it from a phrasebook printed before either of us was born. Splendid, he kept saying. It is splendid you have arrived before the rains. He led me to a room above his family's kitchen, a narrow cell with a plank bed, a window the size of a book, and a view that stopped the breath I had only just recovered: range upon range of peaks receding into a blue haze, the nearest still snow-crowned though it was high summer, the farthest indistinguishable from cloud. That first night I ate tsampa and a fiery pickle of wild greens, drank butter tea that tasted of salt and tallow and, improbably, of comfort, and slept as though I had been struck on the head.

The second day belonged to the mountain and to Dorje's ceaseless, generous commentary. He proposed a walk to the high pasture where his cousin kept yaks through the warm months, and I, having no counter-proposal and no wish for one, agreed. We climbed for three hours along a path that clung to the contour of the slope, crossing streams on slick log bridges, skirting boulders daubed with faded prayers. He named everything. That flower, star-shaped and improbably blue, was medicine for the stomach; that bird, a rufous blur that scolded us from a rhododendron, was a gossip and a thief; that scar on the far ridge, where the green gave way to raw grey rubble, was the memory of a landslide that had taken two houses and, he said, lowering his voice as though the mountain might overhear, four people, in the year his daughter was born. He told me this without visible grief, in the same register he used for the flowers, and I began to understand that up here the catastrophic and the quotidian were not separate countries but neighbors sharing a wall, and that the people who lived among these enormous indifferent stones had made a kind of peace by refusing to rank their sorrows above their small daily pleasures.

The pasture, when we reached it, was a shock of emerald tucked into a fold beneath the snowline, dotted with the black shapes of grazing yaks and a single stone hut from whose roof-hole a thread of smoke rose perfectly straight in the windless noon. Dorje's cousin, a taciturn man with a face even more weathered than his own, greeted us with fresh curd in a wooden bowl and a silence that felt not unfriendly but simply complete, the silence of a man whose companions were animals and altitude. We sat in the doorway and ate, and I watched a hawk ride a thermal in slow ascending circles until it was a speck and then nothing, and I felt the peculiar vertigo of insignificance that mountains induce, that strange lightening of the self, as if some burden I had carried unknowingly up the path had been quietly confiscated at the pasture's edge.

We descended in the failing light, and it was on the way down that the rains Dorje had congratulated me for outrunning finally caught us, sweeping up the valley in a grey wall we could see coming a full minute before it arrived. We sheltered under an overhang while the storm hissed and drummed and turned the path to a rushing rope of brown water, and Dorje, entirely unbothered, produced from somewhere in his coat a small bag of dried apricots and offered them with the air of a man revealing a trump card. We ate apricots and watched it rain and he told me, unprompted, about his son who had gone to the capital to drive a taxi and who sent money and photographs but who could no longer, Dorje suspected, sleep in true silence, having grown accustomed to the machinery of the lowlands. There was no reproach in it, only a father's wonder at the strangeness of what he had produced.

The third morning I woke before dawn without meaning to, roused by the village stirring around me: the creak of a water wheel, the cough of an old man, the flat clank of a bell as someone drove goats up the lane. I wrapped myself in a borrowed blanket and sat at my book-sized window and watched the light come. It arrived slowly and then all at once, the way it does at altitude, first a cold pewter that leached the stars, then a blush that touched only the highest snow and set it burning rose against a sky still nearly black, then a cascade of gold pouring down the ridges and into the terraced fields, waking the colors one by one until the whole valley lay revealed and dripping and impossibly green from the night's rain. Woodsmoke rose from a dozen chimneys. Somewhere a monk or a householder was chanting, low and unhurried, the syllables blurring into the general murmur of a place going about the oldest business there is, which is simply to persist, to plant and winnow and drive the goats up and bring them down, to bury the dead beneath the landslide and marry off the living and send the restless sons away and welcome the occasional foolish stranger who climbs up out of curiosity and stays a little longer than he planned.

I left that afternoon, reluctantly, chased downhill by the threat of worse weather. Dorje walked me to the head of the path and pressed a khata, a thin white ceremonial scarf, into my hands, and told me once more that it was splendid, splendid I had come. I looked back only once from the switchbacks below. He was still standing there, small against the enormous stone, one hand raised, and behind him the roofs and the smoke and the terraces climbing toward a sky that had already begun, at the edges, to grey.
