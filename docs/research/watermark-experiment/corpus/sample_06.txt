Dear Marguerite,

I have been meaning to write to you for weeks now, ever since the great onion catastrophe, which I will get to, but life keeps interrupting itself the way it does, and here I am at last with a cup of tea gone lukewarm beside me and a confession to make. I have taken up cooking. Not the microwave-a-thing sort of cooking I have subsisted on since roughly the Clinton administration, but actual cooking, with a knife and a flame and a cutting board and a growing collection of grievances against recipe writers who assume you know what "sweat the aromatics" means. Reader, I did not. I still am not entirely sure. My aromatics have been sweated, scorched, drowned, and on one memorable occasion set very briefly alight, and I could not tell you which of those, if any, was the intended outcome.

It started innocently, the way these things do. I was tired of the sad rotation of takeout containers colonizing my refrigerator like a small, greasy civilization, and I thought, how hard can it be. People have been feeding themselves for the entire span of human history, most of them without a single YouTube tutorial, and I have a graduate degree. This was, I now understand, the arrogance that precedes a fall. The kitchen does not care about your degree. The kitchen humbles the professor and the fool alike, and it does so with a kind of serene indifference that I have come to find almost spiritual.

The first thing nobody warns you about is the timing. Every recipe is written as though you are a serene octopus, eight arms moving in placid coordination, dicing while stirring while checking the oven while reducing something on a back burner. In reality I have two hands and the working memory of a goldfish, and so my meals arrive in geological strata: the pasta cold by the time the sauce is hot, the vegetables incinerated while I fussed over the protein, everything finishing at wildly different epochs so that dinner becomes less a meal than a series of independent archaeological finds. I once served a stir-fry in which the broccoli was raw enough to squeak and the chicken was, I am fairly confident, structurally identical to jerky. My partner ate it. Bless that saintly soul, they ate it and said it had "character," which is the word people use when they love you but cannot in good conscience say "flavor."

But the onions. You have been waiting for the onions and I will not keep you longer. The recipe, some ambitious French thing I had no business attempting, called for caramelized onions, and it said, breezily, that this would take "about ten minutes." Marguerite, that recipe lied to me the way a friend lies about the length of a hike. Forty-five minutes I stood over those onions. Forty-five minutes, stirring, coaxing, whispering encouragement, while they went from white to translucent to a sort of noncommittal beige and stubbornly refused to become the glossy mahogany jam the photograph had promised. I turned the heat up out of spite. This was my error. They went from beige to black in the span of a phone notification, and the smoke alarm, that shrill Cassandra, began to shriek, and I stood on a chair fanning it with a dish towel while the onions smoldered and the dog hid under the bed and somewhere, I am certain, a French grandmother felt a disturbance and crossed herself. I ordered a pizza. I ate it on the floor. It was the best pizza of my life, seasoned as it was with defeat.

And yet. Here is the strange part, the part I did not expect and cannot quite explain. I kept going. There is something about failing at a thing with your hands, something so much more honest than failing at the abstract things we usually fail at, the emails and the ambitions and the vague sense of falling behind. When you burn the onions, you know precisely what you did wrong. The lesson is immediate and unambiguous and it does not care about your feelings. There is a clean, almost bracing dignity in that. The stove is the least sentimental teacher I have ever had and somehow the kindest, because it never once made me feel stupid for not knowing; it simply let me find out.

I have gotten better. I want to be clear that this is not one of those letters where the writer is secretly a genius fishing for reassurance. I am genuinely mediocre. But I have learned things. I have learned that salt is not a garnish but a load-bearing structural element, and that I spent thirty-nine years underseasoning everything I ever cooked out of some inherited Protestant fear of excess. I have learned that a sharp knife is safer than a dull one, which sounds like a paradox until the dull one slips off a tomato and finds your thumb instead. I have learned to read a recipe all the way through before starting, having once discovered at the eleventh hour that a dish needed to marinate overnight, which is a devastating thing to learn at seven in the evening with a hungry household and rising expectations. And I have learned to taste as I go, constantly, shamelessly, which has transformed cooking from a blind act of faith into something more like a conversation.

There have been triumphs, too, small ones, and I hoard them like a dragon. A roast chicken that came out actually golden and crisp, the skin crackling, the meat improbably juicy, and I stood there staring at it with the disbelieving pride of a parent whose troubled child has unexpectedly graduated. A pot of soup, nothing fancy, just beans and greens and a parmesan rind I had been told to save, that tasted so deeply of something, so much more than the sum of its humble parts, that I ate three bowls and understood, for the first time, why people write poems about food. I texted you a photo of that soup, do you remember? You replied with four exclamation points and I felt like I had won a small war.

What I did not anticipate was how much it would change the shape of my days. There is a rhythm to it, a slowing down. The chopping especially, which I once resented as tedious, has become a kind of meditation, the knife going tap tap tap against the board, the pile of neat little cubes growing, the mind wonderfully empty of everything but the next carrot. In a life spent mostly in front of glowing screens, mostly in my own anxious head, there is something almost radical about a task that requires my body to be exactly where it is. The onion does not exist in the future. The onion is now. I am learning, slowly, from vegetables, to be a person who is where they are.

I think of you often when I cook, honestly, because you were always the one who fed people, who understood instinctively the thing I am only now clumsily discovering, that to cook for someone is one of the oldest and plainest ways we have of saying I am glad you exist and I want you to keep existing, preferably here, at this table, with me. I used to think that was sentimental. I no longer do. I think it might be the whole point.

Come visit, is what I am really saying underneath all this. Come visit and let me cook for you, and I promise the onions will be at worst only slightly burnt, and we will sit at my scarred little table and eat something I made with my own two incompetent hands, and I will tell you the stir-fry story in full, with gestures. Bring wine. Bring the good stories. Bring yourself, which is the only ingredient I have never once managed to substitute.

With love and a faint smell of smoke,
J.
