The fog came up the way it always did in the drowned month, not rolling in off the water but rising from it, as though the sea had made up its mind to stand. Maren had kept the light for eleven winters and she had learned its weathers the way you learn a language you are never asked to speak aloud: by the body, by the small hairs on the arm, by the particular ache that woke in her knee before the barometer had thought to fall. She wound the weight at dusk. She always wound the weight at dusk. The great chain took its slow bite of the cog and somewhere below her the falling counterweight began its patient descent through the tower's stone throat, and above her the lens turned upon its bath of mercury, so massive and so exquisitely balanced that she could stop its whole slow carousel with the flat of one finger, and start it again with a breath. Four flashes, then the dark counting itself out to twelve. That was her signature written on the night. Any captain worth his salt could read it and know precisely which rock was trying to kill him.

She was drinking the last of the tea, bitter and stewed, when the knock came.

It is difficult to convey what a knock is, out there. There was no one to knock. The relief boat had come Tuesday and would not come again for a fortnight; the tide was full and sucking at the landing stage; and no vessel, however desperate, would have closed with the reef in a fog that stood like a wall of wet wool. And yet the knock repeated itself, unhurried, three soft reports against the seaward door, the door that opened onto nothing but a ledge of barnacled granite and a forty-foot drop into the churn.

Maren set down the cup. She was not a fanciful woman. Eleven years of solitude will either fill you with ghosts or scour you clean of them, and she had long since decided which sort of keeper she meant to be. She took the storm lantern down from its hook, unbarred the door, and let the fog walk in.

He stood on the ledge as a man stands on a pavement, quite at his ease, with his hands folded before him. He was young, or had the smoothness of the young, dressed in a coat of some dark oiled stuff that she did not recognize and that shed the wet in beads. His feet were bare. He was not shivering. Most strangely of all, though the fog wept from every surface and her own lashes were already jeweled with it, the man was dry, entirely and impossibly dry, as if he carried some private climate about with him and had merely paused, on his way through the rain of the world, to visit hers.

"You'll be Maren," he said. His voice had an odd cadence, the vowels shaped a fraction long, the way people speak who have learned your tongue faithfully from books and never once heard it in a mouth.

"I'll be whoever's asking," she said. "There's no boat."

"No," he agreed, and looked out at the standing sea with something like tenderness. "There wouldn't be. May I come to the fire? I've come a long way along your light."

She should have shut the door. She knew this even then, with the certainty of a woman who has spent a decade learning exactly which small trespasses the sea will forgive and which it will collect on. Instead she stood aside, because he had said along your light, and no honest confusion could have invented a phrase so precise and so wrong.

He sat where she pointed, in the second chair, the one nobody used, and held his bare hands toward the stove without appearing to need its warmth. Up close his eyes were the flat pale grey of water under cloud, and they moved about the little room, over the log and the ledger and the tide tables and the photograph of a woman she did not explain, with the greed of someone shown a country he had only ever heard described.

"You keep it beautifully," he said. "I want you to know that. The four-and-twelve. We used to argue about your signature, back home. Some said you'd changed it the year the old lens cracked, but I always held you never did. It's the first thing I looked for, coming down. Four, then the long dark. I could have wept."

"Back home," she repeated. "And where is home, that they teach children the flash pattern of my rock?"

He smiled, and the smile was the saddest thing she had seen in eleven years, which is a long time to be alone with the sea and to have accumulated a great catalogue of sad things.

"Under it," he said. "Home is under it. Give or take a hundred years, and a great deal of water." He turned the pale eyes on her. "My light stood where yours stands. The same rock, the same tower, the same falling weight and the same silver bath beneath the lens. I wound it every dusk, as you do. And then the sea came up, the way it is coming up even now, only it did not stop where you have learned to trust it will stop. It took the landing. It took the door we are sitting behind. It took the lamp room last of all, and I stood in the light until the light went out, because a keeper does not leave the light. You know this. It is the one commandment."

The stove ticked. Far above them the great lens went round and round on its mercury, throwing its four spokes of brightness out into a fog that gave them nowhere to land.

"You drowned," she said, because plainness was the only tool she had ever trusted, and she meant to hold onto it.

"I ended," he said, as if correcting a small error of translation. "Keepers of drowned lights don't lie easy. We walk the beams. On the nights the fog stands, when the sea forgets for an hour which century it is in, we come up the light of the towers that are still lit, looking for a window that still burns, and a kettle, and a chair nobody sits in." He glanced at the second chair, at himself in it, and almost laughed. "You'd be amazed how few there are left. You are a rare woman, Maren. Half the coast I knew is a chart of names now. You are one of the last warm rooms on the whole drowned map, and I have been three years of your nights getting here."

She wanted to tell him he was mad, or that she was, or that the tea had been stronger than she'd brewed it. But she was a keeper, and a keeper reads warnings for a living, learns the grammar of things that come to the door out of the dark meaning either to be saved or to save you, and cannot always tell in time which. So she asked the only question that mattered to a woman whose whole life was a light against the water.

"How long," she said. "Until it doesn't stop where I trust it to stop."

He considered her with those grey water eyes, and there was pity in them, and something else, older and more level, the way the tide is level.

"Longer than you," he said, gently. "You'll wind the weight every dusk of your life and die in your bed on the mainland, an old woman who smells of paraffin, and the light will outlast you by a lifetime. I did not come to frighten you. I came because I was cold, and because yours was the last flash pattern I loved before the dark, and a man likes to sit once more in the room he gave his life to. That's all. That's all any of us want. An hour in a warm place that still remembers how to burn."

They sat together until the fog thinned toward morning. He asked her small questions, about the new mantle and the price of oil and whether the cormorants still nested on the west face, and she answered, and it was, she would think for the rest of her long and landbound life, the best conversation she ever had. When the first grey came up honest out of the east he stood, and thanked her, and went out the seaward door onto the ledge where no man could stand, and was not there. On the chair he left a ring of salt water, already drying, and a single coin, worn smooth, stamped with a coastline she could not find in any of her charts.

Maren climbed the stair. She trimmed the wick and doused the lamp against the day. Then she wound the falling weight, though it was not yet dusk and there was no need, wound it to the very top of the tower's throat, because it seemed to her the only argument she had against the water, and she meant to keep making it as long as her arms could turn the crank.
